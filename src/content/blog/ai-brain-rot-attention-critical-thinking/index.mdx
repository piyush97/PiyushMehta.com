You ask AI to summarize an article. Then to explain the summary. Then to write your response. Twenty minutes later, you have a polished opinion you cannot defend without reopening the chat.

**That is the real AI brain-rot question: are you saving time on a task, or skipping the thinking the task was supposed to teach you?**

“Brain rot” is a catchy phrase, not a medical diagnosis. The evidence does **not** show that using ChatGPT permanently damages your brain or gives everyone an eight-second attention span. It does show a more specific risk: when a tool repeatedly does the reading, recalling, and evaluating for you, you may engage less with those parts of the work. Meanwhile, fast answers and endless AI-generated content make it easier to leave a hard question before you have wrestled with it.

## The evidence, without the scary headline

Three studies often get blended into one claim. They measured different things.

The [CHI 2025 study](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778) is especially relevant to office work. Its 319 participants supplied 936 first-hand examples. Higher confidence in the AI's answer was associated with **less self-reported critical thinking**; higher confidence in their own ability was associated with more. The authors also found that thinking did not simply disappear: it shifted toward verifying information, integrating responses, and supervising the task. This was a survey, so it cannot establish that AI _caused_ a decline.

The [MIT Media Lab essay study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872) assigned participants to write with ChatGPT, search, or no tool. Across three sessions, the LLM group showed the weakest EEG connectivity and reported the least ownership of its essays. Many struggled to quote what they had written. In a fourth session, a smaller group switched tools, and prior LLM users still showed lower engagement on some measures when writing unaided. This is a provocative **small preprint about essay writing**, not a diagnosis of atrophy. EEG connectivity is a measure of activity during a task; it is not a measure of someone's intelligence.

And the viral [“LLMs Can Get Brain Rot” paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13928) is about **models**, not people. Its revised version reports that four models continually trained on more low-quality social-media text performed worse on some reasoning and long-context benchmarks. [WIRED's coverage](https://www.wired.com/story/ai-models-social-media-cognitive-decline-study/) describes the earlier experiment. A model trained on junk text is an important data-quality story; it is not an experiment showing that scrolling changes a human brain in the same way.

## Does AI actually shorten your attention span?

We do not have good evidence that ChatGPT use, by itself, permanently reduces a person's general attention span. “Attention span” also is not one fixed number. Staying with a difficult proof, resisting a notification, and recalling a paragraph are different tasks.

The more defensible concern is **fragmented attention during work**. A hard question becomes a prompt. The answer produces five possible follow-up prompts. A notification arrives. You switch from the original problem to evaluating an output, then to chasing another one. Research on [interrupted work](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072) found that interruptions can raise stress even when people compensate by working faster. That study predates modern chatbots; it supports the cost of interruption, not a claim that AI has rewired your attention.

AI can also make the switch away from difficulty unusually attractive. A polished answer arrives before you have formed a hypothesis. If you always choose the easy exit, you get less practice holding a question in mind. That is a **plausible habit mechanism**, not a proven, permanent change in brain capacity.

There is a second route: AI makes new posts, summaries, and clips cheap to produce, so a feed can offer more chances to switch away from a demanding task. [WIRED notes](https://www.wired.com/story/ai-models-social-media-cognitive-decline-study/) the concern that AI-generated slop can feed the next generation of models, too. The possible human attention cost comes from the **feed and the habit of switching**, not from the model-training experiment itself.

One statistic deserves to be retired: the claim that our attention span fell from 12 seconds to eight, below a goldfish's. The [2024 “Brain Rot” review](https://ijrpr.com/uploads/V5ISSUE12/IJRPR36252.pdf) supplied for this article repeats the eight-second figure but attributes it to “Infotainment” without a traceable primary study. It offers no new experiment establishing AI's effect on attention. A vivid number is not evidence because it is easy to share.

## Why critical thinking gets weaker when you let AI go first

Critical thinking is not just spotting hallucinations after the fact. It starts earlier: defining the problem, choosing what evidence would change your mind, and noticing when an answer sounds right but does not fit the situation.

AI can remove precisely those steps:

1. **You skip retrieval.** Instead of trying to remember or reconstruct an idea, you ask for it. The older [“Google effects on memory” experiments](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745) found that people adapt what they remember when they expect information to remain available. That is useful cognitive offloading, but it changes what gets practiced.
2. **Fluency looks like proof.** A neat explanation can make you feel done before you check the premise. In the CHI survey, confidence in AI tracked with less reported critical-thinking effort. The danger is accepting a conclusion because it arrived smoothly.
3. **You inherit the framing.** If the model chooses the assumptions, examples, and trade-offs, your “review” starts inside its version of the problem. Checking its citations is valuable; asking whether it answered the _right question_ is harder.

This matters for software developers. Generating a function is often a good trade. Letting a model decide the failure modes, tests, and architecture before you have made a guess can quietly remove the part that builds engineering judgment. The answer is to keep [AI in the coding workflow](/blog/leveraging-ai-in-coding-workflow/) while retaining ownership of the decision.

The same tool can help you stay with a difficult problem when you use it to organize evidence or attack your first draft. **Who does the first reasoning pass matters more than whether AI appears anywhere in the workflow.**

## A seven-day test: use AI, keep the thinking

This is a workflow experiment, not a treatment for a medical condition. Try it on one recurring task—reading a technical article, debugging, studying, or writing—and notice whether you can explain the result without the chat open.

On day seven, repeat a similar task without AI first. Compare how well you can recall the key points, explain your reasoning, and spot an error. That will tell you more about **your workflow** than a viral “brain rot” score ever could.

## So, is AI rotting your brain?

**No study here establishes literal brain damage or a universal drop in attention span.** The real, testable concern is that effortless answers can displace the mental work that produces understanding. Human studies find lower engagement in some AI-assisted tasks and an association between trust in AI and less reported critical thinking. Neither result means every use is harmful.

Use AI to widen the set of ideas you can examine. Keep the first guess, the hard question, and the final judgment yours. If you cannot explain an answer after closing the chat, the speed was real—but the learning may not have been.

## Questions people ask

### Does ChatGPT cause brain damage?

There is no evidence in the studies above that ordinary ChatGPT use causes structural brain damage. The MIT study measured EEG activity **while participants wrote essays**; its results cannot diagnose injury or permanent decline.

### Can AI reduce critical thinking?

It can reduce the amount of thinking you do **on a particular task** when you delegate its key steps. The CHI survey found an association between greater confidence in AI and less reported critical-thinking effort. It did not prove long-term loss of ability.

### How can I use AI without losing focus?

Start with a brief unaided attempt, ask AI to challenge your reasoning, verify important claims against primary sources, then close the chat and explain the answer yourself. Keep notifications and extra prompts out of one focused work block.
