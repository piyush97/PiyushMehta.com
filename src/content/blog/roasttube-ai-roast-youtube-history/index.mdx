Your YouTube likes are a strange thing to hand to a stranger. [RoastTube](https://roasttube.piyushmehta.com) asks for them anyway. Sign in, let it read the videos you liked, the channels you follow, the playlists you saved, and it will score your taste and print a verdict in the voice of a tabloid court that decided before you arrived.

The court of one jury of one is a joke. The pipeline behind it is not, and most of this note is about that second half: what it took to make a comedy site honest enough that Google would let it keep reading someone's account.

![A sample RoastTube verdict: a Brain Rot Score of 42, an ON THE RECORD stamp, and the three steps from read-only Google access to a written roast.](/blog/roasttube-ai-roast-youtube-history/images/cover.svg)

## A joke with a real pipeline

A roast is assembled from four things. A read-only pass over the visitor's YouTube data. A set of computed signals. A score that is arithmetic. And a model that writes the copy.

<RoastTubePipeline />

Every number on the page is computed, not generated. The signals include the ratio of Shorts to longer videos, topic entropy across categories, a late-night like count, how many likes land on the same channel compared with how many channels the visitor subscribes to, and a handful of contradictions between what someone says they are interested in and what they actually save. The model's job starts after all of that, and it is narrow: turn the numbers and a limited set of titles into a headline, a roast, red flags, per-channel verdicts and a share line, in a shape the page can render.

That boundary is the reason the site can be funny and still be auditable. The joke is language. Everything a reviewer might question is code.

## Google's reviewer found the honest bug

The first verification submission came back with three findings, which I have kept verbatim because they were precise:

- The privacy policy "does not have sufficient content".
- The home page is "behind a login page".
- The home page "does not explain the purpose".

My first instinct was to look for the gate. There was not one. Anonymous requests to the home page, the privacy page and a report all returned 200, and the demo endpoint worked without a session at all. The reviewer still read the page as a login wall, because that is what the page looked like: the loudest element went straight to Google sign-in, the demo was real but unexplained, and the product's purpose existed mostly in courtroom voice.

So the fix was not to remove a gate. It was to stop hiding what was already open:

- Three demo defendants (a lurker, a gremlin, and an essay hoarder) run the entire pipeline with no account, and their verdicts are cached for an hour.
- A plain purpose sentence sits directly under the headline, above anything decorative.
- A three-step strip says what happens in order: sign in with Google read-only, we read your lists and compute the score, the AI writes the roast and the token is discarded.
- The demo grid is labelled as the place to start, the sign-in button says what it is for, and a note under it repeats that the demo above needs no account.
- The privacy page was rewritten from scratch, section by section, against the code.

The useful lesson is not about OAuth. A reviewer reads the home page the way a first-time visitor does, in a few seconds, with no prior context. If the only visible path looks like a login, the product looks like a login, no matter what the anonymous routes return.

## A privacy policy that cites its own source

The rewrite has one rule: every section describes something the code does, and where retention is easy to get wrong, it names the line that decides it. Reports live in Workers KV under a random id and have no TTL, so the policy says a report is kept until deletion is requested, and confirms deletion within 30 days. The counters are different:

| What is stored       | Where                             | How long                                          |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| The finished report  | Workers KV, random 8-character id | Until deletion is requested                       |
| Rate-limit counter   | Workers KV, keyed by IP           | About an hour                                     |
| Daily budget counter | Workers KV, one key per day       | 48 hours                                          |
| Demo report id       | Workers KV, keyed by persona      | 1 hour                                            |
| Google access token  | Sealed HttpOnly cookie only       | Never stored server side, expires within the hour |

Writing that table found two things I had never written down: that a report has no expiry at all, and that deletion is therefore a request a human makes rather than a timer that runs. Both are defensible. Neither was deliberate until the policy forced me to say it out loud.

Two other commitments are load-bearing enough to be structural. The app asks for exactly one scope, `youtube.readonly`, and no refresh tokens, so the token can only ever do one collection pass. The policy carries Google's required Limited Use sentence, and states that Google user data is never used to train models and never shared beyond delivering the roast.

A privacy policy is the cheapest specification you will ever write for data handling. Mine was wrong until the reviewer made me read it.

## Which model? I ran the eval instead of arguing

The obvious free-by-default choice was Workers AI: same vendor as the host, free, and no retention. The obvious quality choice was Gemini, which costs money on the volume a live site sees. Rather than reason about it, I ran three personas through four engines and kept the raw output in the repository: twelve runs, same prompts, same signals.

Only Gemini's native `responseSchema` produced the object the page needs. All three Workers models returned prose even with an explicit JSON contract in the prompt and `response_format` set to a JSON object: red flags and per-channel verdicts were missing, and the score was not parseable as a number.

| Engine                                  | Held the report shape        | Prose                                       | Notes                                                             |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `gemini-3.5-flash`                      | Yes, native `responseSchema` | Tabloid, on voice                           | Primary, with `gemini-flash-lite-latest` as fallback              |
| Workers AI `@cf/openai/gpt-oss-120b`    | No                           | Decent but flat                             | 6 to 27 seconds per run, and one to six raw em dashes to clean up |
| Workers AI `@cf/qwen/qwen3-30b-a3b-fp8` | No                           | Markdown and em dashes leaked into the copy |                                                                   |
| Workers AI `@cf/zai-org/glm-4.7-flash`  | No                           | Chain-of-thought dumped into the output     | 60-second timeouts                                                |

The request that Gemini gets is deliberately boring, which is what makes it reliable:

```json
{
  "generationConfig": {
    "temperature": 1.0,
    "responseMimeType": "application/json",
    "responseSchema": "…"
  }
}
```

The conclusion was not "Gemini is better". It was that `gpt-oss-120b` is the only viable free fallback, and swapping the engine to it is not a one-line change: it needs a server-side schema-repair layer, because the model will not reliably produce the object. That is a real task, and I would rather know it now than discover it during an outage.

Twelve samples is not a benchmark. It was enough to settle the question I actually had, which was whether the free option could carry the contract. It could not.

## The model writes the roast. Code owns the number

The single most useful rule in the codebase is that the model's opinion of the score is discarded. It returns the whole report, including a score field, and the code overwrites it before the report is saved:

```ts
// Hard-compute wins over model self-report.
report.brainrotScore = r.score;
report.brainrotTier = r.tier;
```

Everything the visitor is judged on is computed. Everything the visitor reads is written. Keeping those apart means a garbled number is impossible, and a dull roast is a prompt problem rather than a correctness problem.

Two small guards handle the ways a language model tells on itself. One converts inline labels like `Paragraph 2:` into real paragraph breaks, because the model sometimes narrates its own structure instead of using it. The other removes em and en dashes, which are the loudest tell in generated prose, while keeping hyphens inside number ranges:

```ts
// Em/en dashes are the strongest AI-writing tell. Swap them for commas or a
// sentence break; number ranges keep a plain hyphen. Prompt rules ask the
// model to avoid them already, this guarantees it.
```

That second guard exists because a tabloid that writes like a chatbot is not a tabloid. Voice is a product feature here, so it gets a test-covered function rather than a hope.

## Two failures I did not plan for

The first was cosmetic and total. Every share card for every report returned a blank image, because the CSS parser inside the Cloudflare runtime rejects the `double` border keyword that `ImageResponse` accepts everywhere else. The fix keeps the newsprint look by drawing the double rule as two bars, with a comment so nobody restores the keyword:

```tsx
{
  /* workerd's CSS parser rejects the `double` border keyword in
    ImageResponse (throws, blank card), so the classic double rule is
    drawn as two solid bars. */
}
<div style={{ display: 'flex', flexDirection: 'column', height: 6 }}>
  <div style={{ height: 4, background: INK }} />
  <div style={{ height: 1, background: INK, marginTop: 1 }} />
</div>;
```

The second bit harder. The first production sign-in failed with `Error 401: invalid_client`, and the client id sent to Google was wrapped in literal single quotes. A double-wrapped dotenv value had survived the deploy wizard, which stripped exactly one layer before uploading secrets. The fix was a quote-stripper that removes any number of layers, applied to every value the wizard reads.

Both failures share a cause: the deploy path is product code, and the renderers underneath are not the ones I test against in a browser. The share card had been fine locally for weeks.

## Rationing a paid API on a free tier

RoastTube pays per generation, so the budget is a product decision, not an ops detail. Three roasts per hour per IP, checked before the body is parsed, and a rejected request never writes: hammering the endpoint cannot extend the window. One site-wide counter allows fifteen roasts a day.

The daily counter is a soft limit, and the code says so. Reading and writing the counter is not atomic, so a burst of concurrent requests can overshoot by one, which the margin on the free tier absorbs. I would rather document that than pretend the count is exact.

When a limit is reached, the visitor gets a sentence instead of a status code:

> 3 roasts per hour. Breathe. Try again later.

> The court has heard enough cases today. Come back tomorrow.

The same treatment applies to upstream failures. An expired Google session, a YouTube quota refusal, and a model outage each get their own error code, their own copy and a structured log line with no titles, ids, IP addresses or tokens in it. Failures that are worth watching are worth watching without collecting anything about the person who triggered them.

## Fail closed, then say why

The production defaults are deliberately unfriendly to a half-finished deploy. A missing session secret throws instead of signing sessions with nothing, and a missing Turnstile secret fails closed rather than switching the bot check off. The practical consequence is that a deploy landing before its secrets exist answers 403 to everyone on purpose.

I prefer that to the alternative. An open door is harder to notice than a locked one, and a site that hands out free generation would be found quickly. The cookie handling follows the same instinct: the session is sealed and HttpOnly, and when the rate limiter falls back to `X-Forwarded-For` it takes the last entry rather than the first, because earlier entries are client-controlled and last is what the edge actually saw.

## What is still open

Some honest edges, in the order I would fix them.

The eval is three personas, not a benchmark. It answered the question I had, and it would not survive being quoted as an accuracy claim.

The demo cache hides how the rate limit feels. A visitor's fourth click in an hour meets the wall; a demo click usually does not, because it is served from cache.

Report links are public by nature. Sharing is the point, so a link that only its owner can open was never the goal, but it is a design decision rather than a default.

Fifteen roasts a day is a real ceiling on a free tier, and it exists so that a spike in traffic cannot become a bill. The upgrade path is documented and boring: a paid worker plan plus a paid model tier raises both.

And verification is resubmitted, not approved. Google is currently reading the same home page and privacy policy a reviewer would, which is a useful place for them to be.

## The joke only holds if the boring half is honest

RoastTube is a small entertainment product, and the roast is the part anyone came for. But the reason it works is that the model is confined to the one job it is good at and everything else is ordinary engineering: computed signals, a score in code, one scope, sealed sessions, a budget with a number in it, and error copy a person can act on.

The comedy is what the visitor sees. The pipeline is what makes it safe to show them, and it turned out to be the more interesting thing to build.
