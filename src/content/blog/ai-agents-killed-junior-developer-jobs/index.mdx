Here's a contradiction nobody in tech wants to say out loud: **92% of companies plan to hire this year**, and **entry-level engineering postings have collapsed 20-35% globally** over the same stretch. Google and Meta are hiring roughly half as many new grads as they did in 2021. At Big Tech overall, new grads now make up about **7% of hires**, down from **32% in 2019**.

Both of those things are true at once. That's not a hiring freeze. It's a hiring *rewrite* — and if you're a junior developer, or you manage a team of them, you need to understand exactly what got rewritten, because "learn to code" was never the part that was fragile.

## The jobs didn't disappear. The task list did.

The honest mechanical reason is unglamorous: there is no economic incentive to hire a junior engineer who takes three days to do what an agent finishes in three minutes. Boilerplate, CRUD scaffolding, simple bug fixes, first-draft docs, "implement this well-specified ticket" — that entire category of work is exactly what coding agents are good at today, and it also happens to be exactly the category of work junior engineers used to cut their teeth on. It wasn't busywork. It was the training pipeline. And it just got automated out from under the people who needed it.

Meanwhile the postings that *are* growing tell the other half of the story. AI/ML engineering now leads every hiring category, up **163% year over year**, with 500,000+ open roles globally and roughly **3.4 open AI roles per qualified candidate**. AI skill requirements now show up in **73% of U.S. tech job postings**, up from 71% the month before, and up **192% year-over-year**. Demand for engineers didn't fall. Demand for a specific, cheap kind of engineering labor did.

## Nobody's hiring "coders" anymore. They're hiring supervisors.

Fortune profiled this shift back in March as the rise of what they called **"the supervisor class"** — engineers whose primary value is no longer writing the code, but orchestrating the agents that write it, reviewing what comes out, and making the architectural calls that decide whether the whole system holds together. That's not a euphemism for "manager." It's a genuinely different skill:

- **Context design** — structuring a codebase, its docs, and its interfaces so an agent can actually operate inside it without hallucinating half the API surface.
- **Agent orchestration** — defining roles, tool access, and hand-off rules when multiple agents are working a task in parallel, which is now commanding premium comp precisely because almost nobody can do it well yet.
- **Validation judgment** — knowing *when* AI output is right, when it's confidently wrong, and being able to explain the difference to a security reviewer or a product owner.

Hiring managers have quietly updated the interview script to match. The question isn't "have you used Copilot or Cursor" anymore — everyone has. It's "when do you choose *not* to use it, and how do you verify what it gave you." Engineers who can answer that with a real story are being hired over engineers who can't, at every level, junior included.

## So what do you actually do with this?

If you're early-career, the uncomfortable truth is that the old apprenticeship path — get hired, get assigned small well-defined tickets, build judgment slowly — is narrower than it was three years ago. The counter isn't to avoid AI tools; it's the opposite. Show up already fluent, and spend your visible effort on the 20% of the job an agent still can't do for you: reading a vague requirement and turning it into a real spec, catching the security implication nobody wrote down, deciding whether the fast path is the right path. That's the part hiring managers are explicitly screening for right now, and it's learnable faster than "years of experience" implies.

If you're senior or hiring, the leverage move is the same one that always wins in a platform shift: don't compete on typing speed, compete on judgment. The engineers commanding the new premium aren't the ones who adopted the tools fastest — they're the ones who can tell you, with specifics, where the tools stop being trustworthy.

The market isn't shrinking. It's re-pricing a skill that used to be scarce — writing code — now that it's cheap, and re-pricing a skill that used to be implicit — judgment about code — now that it's the whole job.
